What it does

Every one of these is in the app today. Nothing on this page describes something that is coming later.

📷

Log in seconds

Scan a barcode and it is in. Search by name with the foods you eat most already at the top. Or quick-add the numbers when you already know them. Ate the same as yesterday? Copy the whole day in one tap.

🔬

Numbers you can check

Whole foods come from USDA FoodData Central, built on laboratory analysis. Packaged foods come from the barcode, so the numbers are the ones printed on the packet. When a figure is estimated rather than looked up, the entry says so.

🍳

496 recipes, filtered to you

Every calorie and gram computed from the ingredients, never guessed. Filter by diet, allergens, time, effort and the equipment you own. Scale to the number of people eating, then send the ingredients to a shopping list.

🎯

Targets that learn you

Daylish watches your real weight trend and what you actually eat, works out what you are genuinely burning, and adjusts. Every change comes with a plain-English reason — never a number that appears without explanation.

💡

What fits, right now

The hardest question in any food diary is "so what do I eat now?" Ideas answers it from your own history and the recipe library, ranked against what is left of your day, at a portion already sized to fit.

⏱️

The whole day

Weight trend, 14 days of intake against target, water scaled to your bodyweight, fasting timers for 16:8 through OMAD, and 37 nutrients with 23 vitamins and minerals behind a simple/detailed toggle.

The language model never produces a nutrition number

It is worth being specific about this, because it is the rule the whole app is built around. AI writes recipes. It does not compute calories. Every calorie and every gram in Daylish — in a logged food, in a recipe, in a scaled portion — is calculated from USDA FoodData Central by ordinary code you could audit. A photo-estimating feature was built and then deliberately removed, because the model has to supply the portion mass and calories scale linearly with it: it was the one feature that could produce a confidently wrong number in an app whose entire claim is that it does not.
